**Research Report: Lions (Panthera leo)**  

---

### Executive Summary  
Lions are the most social of all wild felids, living in complex family units called prides that typically consist of related females, their offspring, and a small number of resident males. Their social structure, habitat preferences, and population status vary across Africa and the limited Asian population, with conservation concerns driven by habitat loss and human‑wildlife conflict.  

---

### Key Findings  
- **Social Structure**: Prides are female‑centric; males form coalitions and usually leave the natal pride after a few years.  
- **Group Size**: Average pride size is ~15 individuals, but can range from 3 to 40 depending on prey abundance and habitat.  
- **Habitat**: Historically widespread across Europe, Asia, and Africa; now largely confined to Saharan Africa and the Gir Forest in India (Asian lions).  
- **Population Status**: African lions estimated at ~20,000–25,000 individuals; Asian lions are critically endangered with ~500–600 remaining.  
- **Conservation Threats**: Habitat fragmentation, human‑livestock conflict, and poaching are the primary drivers of population decline.  

---

### Analysis  
Lions’ unique social organization—female‑dominant prides with male coalitions—facilitates cooperative hunting and territorial defense, which are essential for survival in open savanna ecosystems where prey is patchily distributed. The size and stability of a pride are closely linked to prey density; abundant prey supports larger prides, while scarcity leads to smaller, more fragmented groups. In Asia, the isolated Gir Forest population demonstrates how geographic isolation can lead to genetic bottlenecks, exacerbating vulnerability to disease and environmental change. Conservation strategies must therefore address both ecological factors (e.g., prey restoration, habitat connectivity) and human dimensions (e.g., community engagement, conflict mitigation) to ensure long‑term viability of lion populations across their range.
